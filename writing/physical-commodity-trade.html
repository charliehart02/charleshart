What Actually Happens After a Physical Commodity Trade?

Following a physical commodity transaction from execution through logistics, risk and settlement.

CHARLES WILLIAM HART
8 MIN READ

When people think about commodity trading, they tend to picture the moment a trader decides to buy or sell oil, metals or agricultural products. But agreeing the trade is only the beginning.

A physical commodity transaction can trigger a chain of processes involving traders, operations teams, risk managers, counterparties, banks, logistics providers and legal documentation before the commodity and the money ultimately reach their destinations.

1. The trade

Imagine a trading company agrees to purchase a cargo of crude oil. At its simplest, the commercial agreement establishes the commodity and grade, quantity, price or pricing mechanism, delivery location, delivery period and counterparty.

Unlike buying a share on an exchange, physical commodity transactions can contain considerable complexity. The price might not even be known when the trade is executed. It could instead be determined using an agreed benchmark during a future pricing window, perhaps with a premium or discount reflecting the particular crude being traded. And crucially, there is an actual physical product that has to move.

2. Trade capture

Once agreed, the trade needs to be recorded accurately in the firm's systems. This creates the internal record from which many subsequent processes operate. Details such as quantity, price, dates, product and counterparty therefore matter enormously. An error at this stage can flow through multiple downstream processes.

The trader may have made the deal, but the organisation now has to make the deal happen.

3. Confirmation

The two counterparties need to establish that their records of the transaction agree. A trade confirmation records the commercial terms agreed between them. Depending on the product and transaction, these can range from relatively standard documents to significantly more bespoke agreements.

Differences need to be identified and resolved. Perhaps one side has recorded a different quantity, date, pricing term or other economic detail. These discrepancies can appear minor, but an unresolved difference can become considerably more important when money or physical delivery is involved.

4. Risk doesn't end when the trade is executed

The firm now has an economic exposure. Suppose the company has agreed to purchase crude that it intends to sell later. A fall in the value of that crude could reduce or eliminate the expected trading margin.

The trading desk may therefore hedge some of its exposure using derivatives such as futures or swaps. But hedging introduces another consideration: basis risk. The physical commodity and the instrument used to hedge it may not move perfectly together. A cargo of a particular crude grade, for example, is not identical to a Brent futures contract.

5. The commodity has to move

This is where physical commodities become particularly interesting. A financial position can ultimately be represented electronically. A physical cargo cannot. Someone has to coordinate its movement.

For oil, that can involve vessels, terminals, storage facilities, pipelines, inspection and extensive documentation. Metals might involve warehouses and freight networks. Agricultural commodities introduce their own transportation, storage and quality considerations.

Timing matters. Quality matters. Location matters. A profitable trade on paper can become less profitable if transportation costs change, delivery is delayed or the commodity does not meet the required specification.

In physical trading, logistics isn't simply administration surrounding the trade. Logistics can be part of the economics of the trade itself.

6. Pricing and valuation continue

Many physical commodity trades are not executed at a single fixed price. The final price might reference a benchmark over a specified period, with adjustments for factors such as quality, location or transportation.

As market prices change, the value of the position therefore changes too. Firms need to understand their current exposure and resulting profit and loss before the transaction has necessarily settled. This is one reason the distinction between realised and unrealised P&L matters.

7. Settlement

Eventually, invoices are produced and payments have to occur. The amount must correspond with the agreed economics of the transaction, while the appropriate commodity and supporting documentation must have been delivered according to the contract.

Banks and financing arrangements can also become important because physical commodity trading is highly capital intensive. A single cargo can represent a substantial amount of money, and trading firms do not necessarily want that capital tied up for the entire period between purchasing and reselling the commodity.

8. Reconciliation

Even after settlement, firms need to ensure their records agree. Payments, positions and transactions may need to be reconciled between different internal systems and against external records. Any differences need investigation.

The objective is straightforward: what the organisation believes happened should match what actually happened.

One trade, many functions

In reality, these stages aren't always neatly sequential. Several can occur simultaneously, and the exact lifecycle varies significantly between commodities, products and firms.

But that complexity reveals something important about commodity markets. A successful physical commodity trade isn't simply about correctly predicting whether a price will rise or fall. It can depend on pricing, logistics, financing, documentation, risk management and operational execution all working together.

The trade lifecycle turns that commercial decision into reality.
